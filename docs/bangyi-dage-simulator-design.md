## Problem Statement

`榜一大哥模拟器` is a commercial anime-style management and interaction game where the player is not the streamer, but a livestream viewer. The player enters an AI-driven livestream room, completes tasks, spends small amounts of in-game currency, gains status, and eventually becomes the room's top supporter.

The core fantasy is not "paying money." The core fantasy is public recognition and control:

- The streamer notices the player.
- The chat reacts when the player enters.
- Moderators, old fans, haters, and other viewers change behavior around the player.
- The player can influence topics, punishments, challenges, chat rules, and streamer behavior.

This should be positioned as an audience power simulator, not a streamer simulator and not an AI companion app.

## Market Read

Existing references show that livestream and VTuber simulation already have demand:

- `Streamer Life Simulator` and `Streamer Life Simulator 2` validate the broad "streamer life / livestream economy" fantasy on Steam.
- `RE0: VTuber Life` validates anime-style VTuber simulation, community memes, story events, and multiple endings.
- Chinese Steam title `主播模拟器（Streamer Simulator）` already mentions both becoming a streamer and becoming a rich viewer who props up a streamer, which confirms the fantasy is visible to other developers.
- AI companion products such as `Moe-AI` validate interactive anime AI characters, but they sell companionship rather than livestream-room status.

The opportunity is to avoid the crowded "be the streamer" lane and own the opposite perspective:

> I am the viewer whose presence changes the entire room.

## Demand Evidence

The strongest demand hypothesis:

Young viewers who watch chat-oriented streamers already understand the social hierarchy of livestream rooms: top supporters, moderators, old fans, colored names, entrance effects, private groups, and streamer attention. Many can afford small gifts, but cannot afford the real cost of becoming a top supporter.

The game offers a compressed fantasy:

- Spend little or complete tasks.
- Become the person everyone recognizes.
- Watch the streamer and chat react immediately.
- Exercise limited but visible control over the room.

This is stronger than a generic "live streaming is popular" argument because it names a specific unmet fantasy: public status inside a small parasocial room.

## Target User

Primary early user:

- Young players who watch chat-oriented streamers.
- Familiar with streamer rooms, fan ranks, moderators, top supporter lists, and chat rituals.
- Has made or considered small gifts, but cannot become a real top supporter.
- Likes anime-style characters, meme-heavy chat, and semi-romantic attention.
- Wants power, recognition, and chaos more than pure management optimization.

Secondary users:

- Players of anime dating sims and AI companion tools who want more social context than one-on-one chat.
- VTuber fans who understand fan community drama and room culture.
- Short-video viewers attracted by absurd "top supporter enters the room" clips.

Non-target users:

- Hardcore tycoon players expecting deep economy simulation.
- Players who want realistic streaming career management.
- Users looking only for unrestricted AI romance chat.

## Core Product Thesis

The game works if these three premises are true:

1. Players want livestream-room status more than raw donation numbers.
2. AI is valuable because it makes the room react, not because it enables infinite conversation.
3. The first version must create short-video moments within 15 minutes.

If any of these fail, the project should be reconsidered before expanding scope.

## Recommended Approach

Build a 15-minute vertical slice:

> A player enters as a nobody, gains recognition through tasks and small spending, becomes the top supporter, and triggers several room-changing events where the streamer, chat, moderator, old fans, and haters all react.

This combines:

- Approach A: 15-minute top-supporter experience.
- Approach C: viral event collection.

Do not start with the full AI livestream society architecture. That is a second-stage system only worth building after the vertical slice proves the fantasy is fun.

The first playable version should target mobile portrait play. WeChat Mini Game is the best first channel for low-friction testing and sharing, but the underlying event data, UI state, and dialogue system should not depend on WeChat-specific APIs.

## MVP Scope

### Included

- One anime-style chat streamer.
- One livestream room UI.
- One player nickname and visible status level.
- One top-supporter progression track:
  - passerby
  - familiar face
  - room regular
  - moderator candidate
  - top supporter
  - room core
- Three chat personality groups:
  - flatterers
  - jealous old fans
  - haters / rhythm starters
- One moderator character.
- Twenty scripted or semi-scripted high-density events.
- Five power buttons unlocked by status.
- Lightweight memory: the streamer remembers key player choices and calls the player by name.
- AI-assisted responses constrained by event templates and safety rules.

### Excluded From MVP

- Multiple streamers.
- Open world.
- Full dating system.
- Deep economy simulation.
- Real-money gacha.
- Fully freeform command system.
- Complex AI-to-AI long conversation.
- Large wardrobe and Live2D customization library.

## 15-Minute Experience Flow

### Minute 0-2: Cold Entry

The player enters the room as a normal viewer.

Expected feedback:

- Chat ignores the player or lightly mocks them.
- The streamer reads only some messages.
- The player sees top supporter, moderator, and old fan hierarchy.
- A clear prompt shows the first reachable goal: become a familiar face.

### Minute 2-5: First Recognition

The player completes a task or sends a small gift.

Expected feedback:

- The streamer reads the player's name.
- Chat reacts with a few jealous or teasing comments.
- The player's name color or badge changes.
- A small "room influence" meter appears.

### Minute 5-8: Social Friction

The player receives more attention.

Expected feedback:

- Old fans complain that the streamer is reading the player too much.
- A moderator warns the room to stop fighting.
- The player gets the first low-level power: choose the next topic or request a small reaction.

### Minute 8-12: Top Supporter Push

The player completes a challenge, sends a limited gift, or wins a room event.

Expected feedback:

- The top supporter board changes.
- Chat floods with "top supporter is here" style reactions.
- The streamer changes tone and treats the player as important.
- The moderator becomes deferential or defensive.

### Minute 12-15: Power Moment

The player uses a status power that visibly changes the room.

Expected feedback:

- The player chooses a streamer challenge, topic, punishment, or chat rule.
- The streamer performs or reacts in-character.
- Chat splits into factions.
- A short-video-worthy clip naturally emerges.

## Power Buttons

These are not generic commands. Each must produce visible social reaction.

1. Change Topic
   - The player picks what the streamer talks about next.
   - Chat reacts based on topic preference.

2. Name Call
   - The streamer says the player's nickname with a special tone.
   - Old fans react with jealousy.

3. Challenge Card
   - The player requests a small performance, dare, voice line, or mini-game.
   - The streamer can accept, refuse, negotiate, or blush depending on relationship and room mood.

4. Silence the Noise
   - The player can suppress one type of chat for a short time: haters, old fans, or spam.
   - This creates social consequences rather than just removing text.

5. Decide the Punishment
   - In a room event or PK-style scenario, the player chooses the consequence.
   - This is the strongest early "I control the room" moment.

## Event Samples

1. The player enters and no one notices.
2. The streamer misreads the player's nickname.
3. A small gift makes the streamer pause mid-sentence.
4. An old fan says, "New guy thinks he owns the room?"
5. The moderator asks the player to stop spamming.
6. The streamer asks the player to choose the next topic.
7. A hater says the streamer only cares about gifts.
8. The player becomes a familiar face and receives a colored badge.
9. The current top supporter enters and tests the player.
10. The streamer reads the player's message over everyone else's.
11. Chat starts copying the player's catchphrase.
12. The moderator privately asks the player not to cause trouble.
13. The streamer asks whether to sing, chat, play a game, or do a challenge.
14. The player wins a timed room event.
15. The top supporter board updates and chat erupts.
16. Old fans accuse the streamer of changing.
17. The streamer defends the player or dodges the conflict.
18. The player gets temporary moderation power.
19. The streamer asks the player to decide a punishment.
20. The room creates a new nickname for the player.

## AI Design

AI should be used for controlled liveliness, not total freedom.

Recommended structure:

- Hand-authored event state machine controls progression.
- AI generates streamer lines inside bounded situations.
- AI generates chat variations using defined chat archetypes.
- The system tracks room mood, player status, streamer affection, old-fan jealousy, hater intensity, and moderator trust.

Important rule:

The player must feel that AI is reacting to status changes, not merely responding to prompts.

## Safety And Content Boundaries

The product can be suggestive, but it should not rely on explicit content.

Safer commercial framing:

- Power fantasy, not sexual coercion.
- Social recognition, not exploitation.
- Flirtation and streamer performance, not unrestricted commands.
- Clear streamer boundaries: the streamer can refuse, negotiate, tease, or redirect.

Avoid:

- "Pay to force the streamer" framing.
- Real-person streamer imitation.
- Unbounded sexual commands.
- Minor-coded characters.
- Monetization that resembles real predatory tipping.

## Monetization

Recommended prototype model:

- No real-money purchases.
- Use free story currency, task rewards, and simulated gifts.
- Treat "spending" as fiction until the fun and compliance risks are understood.

Recommended commercial model after validation:

- Mobile-first premium unlock, ad-supported demo, or compliant virtual item model depending on channel.
- Cosmetic purchases later: room themes, streamer outfits, chat styles, badge effects.
- Avoid real-money gift loops in the first commercial version unless platform compliance, age rating, and payment design are solved.

Why:

The premise criticizes the high cost of real top-supporter status. If the game reproduces that pressure too directly, it undercuts its own fantasy and creates reputational risk.

## Success Criteria

Prototype success should be measured by behavior, not opinions.

Within 15 minutes, a tester should:

- Laugh or react out loud at least once.
- Understand their room status without explanation.
- Notice the streamer treating them differently.
- Notice chat treating them differently.
- Use one power button and see a room-wide consequence.
- Be able to describe one clip-worthy moment after playing.

Commercial signal:

- 30-50 target users try the vertical slice.
- At least 40% finish the 15-minute slice.
- At least 30% say they would wishlist or buy a polished version.
- At least 10 users independently mention the "top supporter entering the room" fantasy.
- At least 5 users share or ask to record a clip.

## Biggest Risks

1. Fake status does not produce real emotion.
   - Test by watching whether players care when chat reacts to their entrance.

2. AI chat feels generic.
   - Fix by binding AI output to room state and archetypes.

3. The game is perceived as a cheap AI girlfriend product.
   - Fix by centering public room dynamics, not one-on-one intimacy.

4. Scope explodes into streamer life sim.
   - Fix by forbidding open world, multiple streamers, and deep economy in MVP.

5. Content boundary becomes messy.
   - Fix by giving streamer agency and designing powers as social controls, not coercion.

## Distribution Plan

Primary validation channel:

- WeChat Mini Game prototype.
- Private test links and small-group sharing.
- Short-video clips on Bilibili, Douyin, and TikTok-style channels.
- Community testing with livestream and VTuber viewers.

Secondary commercial channels after validation:

- TapTap or native mobile app if the game needs stronger monetization, deeper progression, or more AI control.
- Steam if the project expands into a longer premium narrative sim.

Architecture rule:

- Build as a portable mobile game, not as a WeChat-only app.
- Keep events, state, dialogue, and chat pools platform-independent.
- Isolate WeChat-specific login, sharing, payment, and storage behind adapters.

## The Assignment

Before building the full game, create a 15-minute paper prototype or clickable mockup and show it to 10 people who regularly watch chat-oriented streamers.

Do not ask, "Do you like this idea?"

Ask them to play through the sequence and then answer:

- When did you first feel noticed?
- When did you first feel powerful?
- Which chat reaction felt most real?
- What moment would you clip and share?
- Did anything feel fake, cringe, or uncomfortable?

If they cannot name a specific moment where the room treated them differently, the core fantasy is not working yet.

## What I Noticed

- You started with "榜一大哥" as a money/status fantasy, but the sharper version emerged when you said the top supporter can ask the streamer to do things and has "支配直播间的权利."
- Your strongest user insight was not "直播用户很多." It was that small spenders understand the hierarchy but cannot access the top role.
- Choosing the fourth route means you are optimizing for fast emotional proof and short-video spread. That is the right first test, but only if the power fantasy stays stronger than the flirtation.
